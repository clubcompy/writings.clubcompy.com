The **ClubCompy Flier** Newsletter!

#### Extra! Extra! Read All About It

A subscription to the Flier will supply the reader with type-in computer
programming projects (mostly wholesome games) in a friendly, printed and mailed
newsletter format! Even the youngest reader can use the ClubCompy Flier and
https://clubcompy.com[clubcompy.com] to make programs and bootstrap their
understanding of computers and computer programming.

We will transmit sophisticated technical concepts to kiddo who will soak it all
up like a sponge, prepping them for modern life.

Our product will mimic the success of another my own children have enjoyed:
Kiwi Crates from https://www.kiwico.com[KiwiCo]!

[.small-image]
image::/images/KiwicoScreenshot.png["Sales message on kiwico.com that reads: Every month, make something awesome"]

I have watched my young kiddos' faces light up when a physical package gets
delivered to the house with their name on the label. I also saw that Kiwi Crates
made a quality gift for kids with lasting impact - seemingly a rare thing these
days. Subscriptions to the ClubCompy Flier will be just like Kiwi Crates but for
software. Kids will "put together" pre-assembled games and apps by typing them
in. The ClubCompy Flier will be a great use of free-time, with an endgoal to be
proud of, and very little physical waste!

#### What ClubCompy Will Deliver

Here is our current plan. A monthly subscription will include:

* A mailed hardcopy of the ClubCompy Flier as frequently as we are able to
  produce them
  * initially (2025-2026) expect 1 issue every 6 months, gradually increasing to
    monthly as the business expands and we can get a steady stream of content
* Access for 1 adult and 1 child to https://clubcompy.com[clubcompy.com]
  * Additional kid subscriptions can be tacked onto the base subscription

Here are some of the benefits of ClubCompy Flier and clubcompy.com:

* Gives an introduction to computing as a programmer rather than as a user
* Touches advanced computer science topics and techniques in an unassuming,
ad hoc, beginner-friendly way
* Provides lots of game projects that keep kids engaged and stretch the mind
to use imagination and engaging system thinking
* Favor typing on the keyboard over using mouse to interact with the computer,
typing skills!


#### We Can't Do This Alone

We need the community's help writing articles for the ClubCompy Flier. We will
invite software and accompanying articles to be written by enthusiastic users
for which we will pay a modest commission - think $75-$250 in 2025 dollars. As
we will have a dearth of content at the beginning, we will roll over what moneys
we take in each month to pay for articles every month bounty-style, so early
article submissions that make it into the Flier may end up paying more.

That will only pay for some of your doughnuts and coffee, but it is very cool
to share your coding and writing gifts with the world and see your name
in print. We'd appreciate getting your help with quality articles and landing
more subscribers to the Flier.

As mentioned earlier, we're planning to start with two issues of the ClubCompy
Flier in the first year in a modest newsletter format and printing technology.
As we get lots of subscribers and high quality submissions, we'll be able to
hire more help and work the business up to support a monthly subscription with
improved printing and binding quality.

#### Some Background

I made a discovery on Christmas Eve 2008 that led to ClubCompy. There was an
item on Hacker News that gave a
https://web.archive.org/web/20080827215649/http://developer.mozilla.org/en/Canvas_tutorial[tutorial on the new canvas tag] that Mozilla had recently added to Firefox to reach feature parity
with Safari. I suspected canvas represented a massive evolution to the browser
operating environment, which was previously document-centric. Now it was becoming
a general purpose development platform, like an operating system.

My very first inclination was to recreate the Pet-SCII capital letter A character
from the old Commodore 8-bit computers since I could work out the outline of it
from memory:

[.tiny-image]
image::/images/Pet-SCII_A.png["Pet-SCII Letter A"]

By the end of the night, I had my virtual 320x240 screen filled with big chunky
A's. I had only rudimentary HTML, CSS and JavaScript knowledge at the
time, but just with that proof-of-concept I knew I would be dropping everything
and making a computer emulator or simulator or ... something.

By May 2009, there was a high level system design for the multi-planar character
graphics system with double buffered page flipping, parallax scrolling, and
sprites that has largely held up since. It was designed for compatibility with
IE6 and modern HTML5 browsers like Firefox and Chrome. Google had produced a
klunky canvas tag polyfill for IE that allowed us to map our draw commands to
byzantine https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_Markup_Language[VML] tags.

While the IE version was sub-10 FPS, it worked and it could grind through
colorful graphical game frames with an identical look and feel to modern
browsers. Mad props to the original IE team!

On modern browsers, the graphics felt so authentic.  When the screen scrolled
by rows of characters, the display had that clockwork clickity-clickity motion
just like a classic 8-bit computer had.  On IE, it was slow enough that one
could watch the screen draw. It was still charming but not pleasant to work
with.

I knew some retro-flavored home computer simulator would be the goal without
being faithful to any classic machine; it would be a fantasy machine. Things
that a new learner would interact with intensely at first would be simple:
8- or 16-bit graphics, BASIC-like language, simple FM sound. But the underlying
computer would be a modern 32-bit or 64-bit with full memory and math
capabilities. Basically a retro-styled computer with some gentle obstacles here
or there, but when kiddo explores beyond that, the sky's the limits! AND THIS,
my friends, would be an epic learning tool for kids.

By the end of 2009, we had discovered
https://jscc.brobston.com/documentation/introducing/welcome.html[JSCC], a pure
JavaScript parser generator, and we used it to make our BASIC-like language, now
called Comfy. Many parts of it were a simple abstraction over JavaScript, so it
performed decently.

In 2010 https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Gamepad_API[Gamepad]
and in 2011,
https://web.archive.org/web/20110604061057/https://developer.mozilla.org/en/Introducing_the_Audio_API_Extension[Firefox's streaming Audio] API's
dropped. Along with Local Storage and AJAX, the browser was now a complete
environment for games, a huge milestone and legacy tech like Flash went into
full retreat.

We built low-power rack mounted computers and got hosting for clubcompy.com!
The site was up and even got featured once on Hacker News while I was on vacation
in Hawaii! It was such an exciting time.

And then, right after launching a Kickstarter to create a business out of
ClubCompy, we also kickstarted a family.
